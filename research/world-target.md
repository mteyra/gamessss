# World & tradition / Aim & throw — research notes

Research date: 14 November 2026. Dataset: `site/data/world-target.json`, 24 entries, 12 per family.

This is a practical selection of physical games, not a ranking of cultures or a claim to contain every tradition. Places describe strong associations. They do not establish where a game was first invented. Native names and aliases are retained where useful for further discovery. Suggested ages, durations, headcounts and space categories are editorial hosting estimates, not federation prescriptions.

## Selection rationale

The world chapter connects twelve different ideas: handing a chase to a seated player; raiding and returning; rebuilding a destroyed target; striking a feather with a hand; a net rally without hands; asymmetrical circular-field objectives; making a stick jump before batting it; paired batting and running; a rhythmic prediction duel; search followed by a return race; collaborative shuttle kicking; and many runners responding to one kick. These differences matter more than accumulating near-identical titles.

The target chapter ranges from a hit-triggered relay to returning wooden targets, exact-number skittles, proximity bowls, cancellation scoring, assisted disc deflection, wrapping projectiles, shared hoops and successive-landing navigation. Pétanque and bocce remain separate because fixed-circle throwing and lane-based rolling create different play, although both share proximity scoring.

## Specific accuracy decisions

- **Flunkyball:** The traditional adult drinking context is stated honestly. Speed drinking, including water, is not the activity offered. The token relay is designed material and labeled as such.
- **Kī-o-rahi:** The source explicitly describes multiple versions. The atlas preserves Māori terminology and acknowledges local practice rather than calling one short summary universal or official.
- **Peteca:** The inspected English source contains older scoring and notes about later tournament formats. The entry chooses a declared introductory scoring system, retaining only the stable one-touch, hand-strike mechanic as documented core.
- **Sepak takraw:** The Canada page’s operative rules use 15 points, extended to 17 from 14–14; some later example scripts on that same page retain older 21-point scores. The entry follows the operative scoring section and does not copy the stale script.
- **Bete-ombro, Dibeke, Kabaddi:** Changed contact or elimination mechanics are marked house adaptations, rather than being passed off as authentic full rules.
- **Lippa and Gorodki:** Their traditional projectiles require specialist space and handling. The entries explain the actual object mechanic and clearly identify soft practice substitutions.
- **Sources:** General web result pages and a sports-encyclopedia cluster were useful discovery routes, but federation, educational and local cultural sources were favored for actual game entries. No unsupported ancient-origin or worldwide-participation statistics were adopted.

## Verification and scope limits

The JSON has 24 unique IDs, all required fields, 4–5 ordered steps per game, clear endings, at least one source per entry, and the four requested world/target spotlights. Recommended ranges are broad filters: exact team configurations are still stated in the setup. This is desk research and editorial preparation; these teaching formats have not been field-tested with a live group. Formal competition needs the relevant complete rulebook, and local traditional games deserve local knowledge alongside written references.
