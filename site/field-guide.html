01 / WHY THIS EXISTS

Your next favourite game
is someone else’s old favourite.

So much of what we play depends on who happened to teach us. A gym teacher, a neighbour, a cousin at a family gathering. This atlas gives those discoveries somewhere to meet.

The first edition brings together 72 physical games and variants across six families. Some need a full field and equipment. Others need a circle of people and nothing else. Islands, Manhunt, Flunkyball and the many ways to rescue a dodgeball teammate were the starting point.

The selection follows interesting mechanics: passing a chase, reversing a role, rebuilding a target, changing what “out” means. It is an invitation to explore, with enough detail to try a first session.

Places are connections. A country or region on a game card describes an association with that game. It does not establish an exclusive origin. Games move, borrow, split, combine and acquire new names.

02 / A LITTLE KEY

Know which rules you’re reading.

Every game has a setup, a playable sequence, an ending, a variation and source links. The rules label tells you how to interpret the version offered here.

THREE KINDS OF RULE NOTE
LabelWhat it means
Documented gameThe core sequence follows a cited description. A card remains a concise guide; a competition may require additional rules.
Starter rulesA practical introduction with the main mechanic intact. The note identifies shortened rounds, flexible setups or locally variable details.
House adaptationA version deliberately changed or designed for this atlas, or inspired by the original brief. Sources support the parent game; the change is identified.

Headcounts, ages and minutes are hosting suggestions. They are useful for narrowing the list, not official eligibility requirements. Check the actual setup for exact teams. A listed player range may require partners, equal teams or rotation.

Space is relative. Small means a generous circle or short throwing lane; medium means a clear court or lawn; large means room to run with clear boundaries and run-off space. Adjust the real layout to the group and equipment.

Diagrams teach the idea. The atlas uses schematic player positions and movement. Mark your actual playing area before starting; use source diagrams when a sport depends on precise zones or a hoop route.

03 / TAKE IT OUTSIDE

Teach one good round.

  1. Show the objective first. “Get home without a tag.” “Rebuild this tower.” Let players see the thing they are trying to do before explaining every exception.
  2. Walk through a complete turn. Demonstrate the start, one success, one out or mistake, and the restart. A game often falls apart because nobody knows what happens next.
  3. Agree boundaries and a stop signal. Point to each boundary. Try the stop call once while people are moving, so its meaning is shared.
  4. Play a short practice round. Pause after two minutes. Resolve the question that actually came up, then restart cleanly.
  5. Rotate the interesting jobs. Cat, mouse, keeper, thrower, raider, caller: make sure those roles travel around the group.
  6. Finish while there is energy for another. Use the stated ending or an announced timer. Ask which single rule the group would like to try changing next.

A useful first session: a short circle game, one chase or team game, then something with a target. Leave time between them to explain and reset. A saved lineup gives you a starting order; the group sets the pace.

04 / MORE WAYS IN

Keep the interesting decision.
Change what gets in the way.

The heart of Islands is handing over the chase. It can still work with standing pairs. The heart of a target game is choosing a shot. That choice can stay interesting with a shorter lane or a rolling ramp.

  • Movement: try walking, rolling or smaller areas; use fixed positions when a relay would exclude someone.
  • Equipment: choose lighter, softer, larger or more visible objects. Allow control before speed.
  • Communication: pair spoken calls with a raised hand, a colour or a clear gesture. Demonstrate as well as describe.
  • Participation: offer quick re-entry, rotating roles or cooperative totals. Agree touch and comfort before choosing a contact game.
  • Challenge: change one variable at a time. Move a target, add a rescue, reduce touches or make one role temporary.

Check the “Make it work” note on each game for the practical adjustment that matters most. For unfamiliar cultural games, a local player or practitioner can teach things a short written guide cannot.

05 / BEHIND THE CARDS

Curated for discovery.
Written for playing.

Research began with the named games in the brief, then expanded by game family. Federation rules, educators’ activity guides, community organisations and cultural sources were preferred. Variants were chosen for a meaningful change in play, not just a different title.

Each entry links its evidence and states the version being taught. Where sources disagree, the card fixes a clear starter convention and names the difference. Descriptions are original summaries. No popularity score, “world’s best” ranking or precise origin claim was invented.

The Flunkyball card is a useful example: its familiar bottle-and-reset structure comes from a documented adult drinking game; the playable token relay is an explicitly designed alcohol-free adaptation. It preserves the racing decisions without making drinking the activity.

This is desk research and design, dated November 2026. The atlas’s teaching adaptations have not been tested with every age, venue or group. Formal competition and local traditions may use details beyond these introductions.

07 / KEEP THE GAME GOING

Made to be passed along.

The interactive approach draws inspiration from the many renditions in the Aloha Robotics repository: clear scenes and hands-on exploration. Here, every game is explained through clickable animations that show its rules, one move at a time. You can pause, replay or skip to another game.

The games belong to their communities, players, educators and rule-making organisations. Their names identify the activities being discussed. Source links keep those connections visible.
